Diagnosing the Problem

Common Faults and What They Mean

Most boiler breakdowns fall into a handful of categories. Knowing which one you're dealing with helps us get to your door with the right parts, and helps you decide how urgent the job is.

No Hot Water but Heating Works

This is one of the most common combi boiler faults we see on Wirral. It usually points to a diverter valve that isn't switching properly, or a plate heat exchanger that's partly blocked. Both are straightforward to diagnose with the right tools, and in most cases the part can be replaced in a single visit.

Losing Pressure

A boiler that keeps losing pressure, even after you've topped it up, is telling you something is leaking — or that the expansion vessel or pressure relief valve has failed. A one-off drop after bleeding a radiator is normal. Pressure that keeps falling every few days is not, and it's worth having it looked at before it triggers a full lockout.

F-Codes and Lockouts

Fault codes vary by manufacturer, but they're usually the boiler protecting itself — from a failed ignition, a faulty sensor, low water flow, or a pump problem. Worth noting the exact code and any display message before you call; it saves time on diagnosis and means we can often bring the likely part with us.

Kettling and Banging

A kettling or banging noise from the boiler or heat exchanger is almost always a sign of restricted water flow caused by a build-up inside the system. On Wirral, that build-up is nearly always sludge rather than scale — more on why further down this page.

Pilot or Ignition Failure

Older boilers with a standing pilot light can suffer from a worn thermocouple or a draught blowing the flame out. Newer boilers with electronic ignition tend to lock out after repeated failed ignition attempts, often caused by a faulty spark electrode, ignition lead, or gas valve. Either way, this isn't something to keep resetting repeatedly — get it looked at.

Before You Call

Try These Three Checks First

Sometimes the fix takes two minutes and doesn't need an engineer at all. Worth ruling these out before you book a call-out.

1

Repressurise the System

If the pressure gauge reads below 1 bar, most boilers have a filling loop you can use to top it back up to around 1.5 bar, following the instructions on the case or in the manual. If it drops again within a day or two, that's a leak — not something to keep topping up.

2

Check the Thermostat and Timer

A "dead" boiler is sometimes just a thermostat set too low, a timer programmed incorrectly, or flat batteries in a wireless stat. Confirm the demand is actually reaching the boiler before assuming it's broken.

3

Look for a Lockout Reset

Most boilers have a reset button, and it's fine to try it once. If it fires up and runs normally, fine. If it locks out again straight away or repeatedly, stop resetting it and get it looked at — repeated resets can mask a fault that's getting worse.

If you smell gas, or suspect a carbon monoxide leak, don't attempt any of the above — turn off the gas at the meter, ventilate the property, and treat it as an emergency. See our emergency plumber page for what to do next.

An Honest Threshold

Repair or Replace?

Not every fault is worth repairing, and we'll tell you straight when that's the case rather than patching an old boiler for the sake of a call-out fee. A few things we weigh up:

  • Age — most boilers are designed for a working life of around 10-15 years. A major component failure on a boiler already past that point rarely makes financial sense to repair.
  • Cost of repair versus a new unit — if a repair is going to cost a large proportion of what a new, like-for-like boiler installation would cost, it's rarely the sensible option, especially on an older appliance likely to throw up further faults.
  • Parts availability — some older or discontinued models have parts that are hard to source or no longer manufactured, which can turn a simple fix into a long wait.

If replacement is the better option, we'll explain why and talk you through what a new installation involves — see our boiler installation page for details.

Local Knowledge

Soft Water on Wirral: Why Sludge, Not Limescale, Is the Local Cause of Boiler Faults

A lot of generic boiler advice online talks about limescale as the main enemy of a heating system. On Wirral, that's largely the wrong problem to worry about. Wirral and the wider Merseyside area sit on soft water — typically around 73ppm, well below the UK average of roughly 230ppm — so scale build-up inside pipework and heat exchangers is much less of an issue here than it is in hard water regions further south and east.

What we see instead is sludge. Central heating sludge, technically magnetite, forms when the steel and iron components of a heating system slowly corrode from the inside. In a soft water area this corrosion happens more readily, and over years it produces a black, sticky sediment that settles in radiators, pipework, and the boiler's heat exchanger. The result is the kettling and banging noises we cover above, cold spots at the bottom of radiators, a boiler working harder than it should, and premature pump and valve failures.

Because the failure mode here is corrosion sludge rather than scale, the fix is different too — inhibitor levels and system cleanliness matter more on Wirral than a scale-focused water softener would. A power flush clears existing sludge out of the system, and keeping up with a proper annual boiler service — including a magnetic filter check and inhibitor top-up — is the single best thing you can do to stop it building back up.

Common Questions

Boiler Repair FAQs

My boiler has lost pressure — can I fix it myself?

Often, yes — for a one-off drop, most boilers have a filling loop that lets you top the pressure back up to around 1.5 bar following the instructions on the boiler casing. What you shouldn't do is keep topping it up every few days: that's a sign of a leak somewhere in the system, or a failing expansion vessel or pressure relief valve, and it needs a proper look rather than a repeat top-up.

How much is a boiler repair call-out on Wirral?

We don't publish a fixed figure because it depends on the fault, but every job is priced upfront and agreed with you before we start — no surprises on the invoice. Give us a call and describe what the boiler is doing; we can usually give you a good steer over the phone and confirm the exact price once we've diagnosed the fault in person.

Can you get parts for an older boiler?

Usually, yes, for the major brands we work on — Worcester Bosch, Vaillant, Baxi, Ideal and Glow-worm all keep parts available for a long time after a model is discontinued. Some older or less common models can be harder to source parts for, and in those cases we'll tell you honestly if it's likely to be a wait, or whether replacement makes more sense.

At what point is a repair not worth it?

As a rough guide, if the boiler is past 10-15 years old and the repair involves a major component — a heat exchanger or main PCB, for example — it's often close to what a new, like-for-like installation would cost. At that point we'll say so plainly rather than patch it up. Age, the size of the repair bill, and parts availability are the three things we weigh up.

Do you charge extra for evenings or weekends?

We're honest about this: R.J. Sayle is a one-person operation, so we can't guarantee availability outside our normal hours of Monday to Friday, 07:30-18:00. That said, emergency callouts are considered where possible, subject to availability at the time — call and ask, and we'll tell you straight whether we can help.
