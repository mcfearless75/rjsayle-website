Converting to a Combi Boiler on Wirral
— Is It Right for Your House?

A combi conversion suits plenty of Wirral homes — and is a poor fit for others. Below we explain honestly when it works, when it doesn't, and how to find out which camp your house falls into before you spend the money.

When a combi is the right call

Combi boilers make the most sense in smaller households with modest hot water demand. If you're a couple or small family with one bathroom, a combi gives you instant hot water on demand without the wait for a cylinder to reheat, and you never run out mid-shower the way you can with a stored system that's been drained by a bath and a wash.

A combi is also a strong option if your incoming mains water supply has decent pressure and flow rate — enough to feed a shower and a tap running at the same time without either dropping noticeably. And if you're renovating and want the airing cupboard or loft space back — no cold water storage tank in the loft, no cylinder taking up a cupboard — a combi conversion delivers that space gain in one job.

For a lot of Wirral terraces, semis, and smaller family homes with one bathroom, this is exactly the profile where a combi conversion pays off in convenience and reclaimed space.

When it isn't

This is the part most companies skip, so we'll be direct about it. A combi draws all its hot water straight through the boiler's heat exchanger in real time — there's no stored reserve to fall back on. That means a combi can only supply one outlet at full flow at a time. If your household regularly has two showers running simultaneously — a common pattern in houses with two bathrooms and more than one person getting ready in the morning — a combi will struggle to keep both running at a decent flow and temperature. A system boiler with an unvented cylinder stores hot water under mains pressure and can feed multiple outlets at once without that trade-off.

Poor mains flow rate is the other big one. A combi's output is capped by what your incoming mains supply can actually deliver — no amount of boiler power fixes a slow mains feed. If your street has historically weak flow, or you're on a long or narrow supply pipe, a combi may simply never give you a strong shower, regardless of the boiler's rated output.

Large detached properties with high simultaneous hot water demand — several bathrooms, a utility room, guests to accommodate — are also often poorly served by a combi. The demand across a bigger house tends to outstrip what any single combi unit can deliver on tap, and you're more likely to end up frustrated with lukewarm water at the far end of the house than pleased with the space you've saved. In these cases we'll usually recommend against a combi conversion, not for it.

How to check your mains flow rate before deciding

Before committing to a conversion, it's worth checking your mains flow rate yourself — it takes five minutes and tells you a lot. Turn off any other taps and appliances in the house, then run the kitchen cold tap (which is usually fed directly from the mains) into a bucket of known volume for exactly 6 seconds, timed with a phone. Measure the litres collected and multiply by 10 to get litres per minute.

As a rough guide: 18 litres per minute or more is good and should comfortably support a combi, including a decent shower. Between 10 and 18 litres per minute is workable but will limit you to a lower-output combi and a more modest shower experience. Below 10 litres per minute, a combi is unlikely to perform well, and a system boiler with a cylinder — or a boosted cold water system — is usually the better route.

Flow rate and mains pressure are not the same thing, and a common mistake is checking one and assuming it tells you about the other. It's easy to have reasonable pressure but low flow, particularly on older or narrower supply pipes. If you're not confident doing this test yourself, we can measure it properly as part of a free, no-obligation survey and give you an honest recommendation based on the result — not just what's easiest to sell.

What's involved

A combi conversion is a bigger job than a like-for-like boiler swap, and it's worth understanding what it actually entails. First, the cold water storage tank(s) in the loft and the hot water cylinder — usually in an airing cupboard — are removed. That reclaims meaningful storage space, but it also means capping off the old feed and vent pipework correctly so nothing is left live or prone to leaking.

Second, new pipework routes are needed to bring mains cold water directly to the new combi and to reroute hot water distribution around the house from the boiler rather than from a cylinder. Depending on where the new boiler is sited relative to the old cylinder and tanks, this can mean running new pipe through floors, walls, or ceiling voids — the amount of disruption depends on your specific layout.

Finally, we check the existing gas supply pipework and meter capacity. Combi boilers often have a higher instantaneous gas demand than the system boiler or back boiler they're replacing, so the incoming gas supply needs to be confirmed as adequate — or upgraded — before installation, as part of the Gas Safe process.

The alternative — system boiler + unvented cylinder

If a combi isn't the right fit for your household, that doesn't mean you're stuck with an old-fashioned gravity-fed system. A system boiler paired with an unvented hot water cylinder gives you mains-pressure hot water at every outlet — strong showers, no loft tank — while still storing enough hot water to run two bathrooms at once without a drop in performance. You lose a little more cupboard space than a combi would give you back, but you gain genuinely reliable simultaneous hot water, which matters far more in busier households.

We install and service unvented cylinders regularly across Wirral, and for two-bathroom households, low mains flow properties, and larger homes, it's usually the option we end up recommending over a combi. See our unvented cylinder installation page for more detail on how that system works.

What it costs

We won't quote a fixed price here, because a combi conversion genuinely varies more than a like-for-like boiler installation. The cost depends on how far pipework needs to run, how accessible your loft and airing cupboard are, whether the gas supply needs upgrading, and what condition your existing pipework is in. It's always going to cost more than swapping an existing combi for a new one, simply because of the extra pipework and removal work involved.

The only honest way to give you an accurate figure is a site survey, where we check your mains flow rate, look at your existing pipework routes, and confirm gas supply capacity. From that we can give you a fixed, itemised quote with no surprises later. See our boiler prices guide for a general sense of how we price installations, or call us to book a free survey.

Common Questions

Combi Conversion FAQs

Will I lose water pressure with a combi?

A combi runs at your mains pressure, so if your mains pressure and flow rate are good, you'll typically get a strong, consistent shower — often better than an old gravity-fed system. If your mains flow rate is weak to begin with, a combi won't improve on that, because it has no stored reserve to draw on. This is exactly why we check flow rate before recommending a conversion.

How long does a conversion take?

A straightforward conversion in a house with reasonably accessible pipework typically takes two to three days. Where pipework runs are longer, access is more difficult, or the gas supply needs upgrading, it can take longer. We'll give you a realistic timescale once we've surveyed the property.

What happens to my old tanks and cylinder?

We remove the cold water storage tank(s) from the loft and the hot water cylinder from the airing cupboard, cap off the redundant pipework safely, and take the old equipment away for responsible disposal. That leaves you with the reclaimed space ready to use.

Can I get a combi if I have two bathrooms?

You can, but if both bathrooms are used at the same time regularly — for example two people showering before work in the morning — a combi will struggle to keep both running well, since it can only supply full flow to one outlet at a time. In that situation we'd usually suggest a system boiler with an unvented cylinder instead, which can serve multiple outlets simultaneously at mains pressure.

Is a combi cheaper to run than a system boiler?

A combi avoids the standing heat loss of a stored cylinder, which can make it more efficient for smaller households with modest hot water use. For larger households using more hot water throughout the day, a well-insulated unvented cylinder heated on a timer can be just as efficient in practice. Running costs depend more on your household's usage pattern than on the boiler type alone, so we're honest with customers rather than assuming a combi automatically saves money.
